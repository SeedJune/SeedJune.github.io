## Culture as Politics: "Poi E," *Patu!* and the Question of National Culture in 1980s New Zealand

In our group presentation, I examined the social and cultural side of New Zealand in the 1980s. I focused on the 1984 song "Poi E" and Merata Mita's 1983 documentary *Patu!*. Both works reached the public through unusual routes. Record companies and commercial radio showed little interest in "Poi E." *Patu!* faced problems with access to television footage, police interest in its film material, and a long delay before a national television broadcast.

These cases show that national culture was not a fixed idea. It was shaped by audiences, artists, broadcasters, funding bodies, museums, and the police. These groups did not always agree. This report therefore makes a narrower argument than the presentation. Public institutions did not follow one policy towards Māori culture. Their different responses show that the place of Māori voices in national culture was still being contested.

### 1. "Poi E": public success and limited institutional support

"Poi E" came from a town facing a serious economic crisis. The freezing works at Pātea closed in 1982. It had been the economic base of the town for about a century. Its closure caused unemployment and population loss. The Pātea Māori Club wanted to strengthen local pride. Ngoi Pēwhairangi wrote the lyrics, and Dalvanius Prime composed the music. The song combined poi and waiata with funk, synthesisers, and breakdancing. It was sung entirely in te reo Māori.[^1]

The form of the song was important. It did not present Māori culture as something that belonged only to the past. The music video placed kapa haka, young breakdancers, local streets, and everyday life in the same frame. Traditional and modern elements supported each other. This helped the song speak to younger Māori listeners while also reaching a wider national audience.

The music industry did not predict that response. New Zealand record companies were not interested, so Prime formed Maui Records. Commercial radio gave the song little airplay. A story on the television programme *Eyewitness* helped it reach a larger audience. "Poi E" then spent four weeks at number one and twenty-two weeks on the charts. It became the highest-selling single in New Zealand in 1984.[^2]

When the club was invited to perform in Britain, money was again a problem. In the later documentary *Poi E: The Story of Our Song*, Prime recalled that he mortgaged his house so the group could travel. Some later accounts describe a rejected request for public funding. However, the sources I consulted do not clearly identify the agency or preserve the wording of its decision. This episode should therefore be treated as evidence of limited official support, not as proof of a formal government policy.[^3]

The historical significance of "Poi E" lies in the gap between industry judgement and public demand. A te reo Māori song succeeded without normal support from record labels or commercial radio. This does not mean that the song caused the Māori Language Act 1987. Māori activism, language education, and the Waitangi Tribunal were more direct influences. The song still showed that a national audience for te reo already existed before Māori became an official language.[^4]

### 2. *Patu!*: film-making, evidence, and public memory

In 1981, the South African Springbok rugby team toured New Zealand while apartheid remained in force. The tour divided the country. More than 150,000 people joined over 200 demonstrations in 28 centres, and about 1,500 people were charged. The protests became one of the largest periods of civil disorder in modern New Zealand history.[^5]

Merata Mita organized a film project to record the anti-tour movement. Many camera operators contributed their time and footage. The material came from different cameras and film stocks, which made editing difficult. The production had limited resources, but it was not completely without funding. NZ On Screen records a small budget, including an $18,000 loan from the New Zealand Film Commission and support from the Arts Council and Catholic groups. This is important because it shows that public institutions did not act as a single bloc.[^6]

Other institutions created serious obstacles. Television networks refused access to their tour footage. Film stock was moved and hidden when the production team feared police seizure. The police sought material that could be used as evidence against protesters. The production team also had to protect the material while the film was being completed. These events made the production process political. The dispute was not only about what happened on the streets. It was also about who could keep, edit, and interpret the visual record.[^7]

The film itself is a primary source with a clear position. Its close street-level footage places the viewer among the protesters. It records police lines, confusion, fear, and violence from the perspective of people opposing the tour. This viewpoint is one of its strengths, but it is also a limitation. *Patu!* does not try to give equal space to both sides. It is an activist documentary, not a neutral news report.

*Patu!* premiered at the 1983 Wellington Film Festival and received a standing ovation. It also screened at independent venues and international festivals. However, it did not reach a national television audience until 1991. The delay matters because television was a major source of public information. For many viewers, one of the fullest visual accounts of the protests was absent from television for ten years after the tour.[^8]

The film's significance therefore goes beyond its subject. It preserved a counter-record of 1981 and challenged the idea that official or mainstream coverage was complete. It also shows that historical evidence is produced under pressure. What survives depends on access to cameras, money, archives, and distribution.

### 3. Reading the cases together

The comparison becomes more complex when *Te Māori* is added. This major exhibition of taonga opened at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York in September 1984. Cabinet had approved the project in principle in 1981. A government management committee helped organize it, while a Māori subcommittee and iwi representatives guided cultural decisions. Māori leaders travelled with the taonga, and cultural ownership remained with Māori communities.[^9]

*Te Māori* should not be described as a safe version of Māori culture created by the state. It depended on Māori leadership and changed museum practice. It also gained strong official and corporate support. By contrast, "Poi E" first succeeded through local organization and public demand. *Patu!* received some public funding, but it also faced resistance from police and television institutions.

The pattern is therefore uneven rather than simple. Public institutions could support Māori culture in one setting and resist it in another. Taonga presented overseas as art and heritage attracted national support. Contemporary Māori popular music had to prove itself in the market. A documentary that criticized racism and police conduct had a more difficult path to mass broadcasting. These differences suggest that national culture was being negotiated through decisions about funding, access, and circulation.

### Reflection on sources

NZ On Screen was the most useful resource for my topic. It gave me direct access to the "Poi E" music video and the full version of *Patu!*. These are primary audiovisual sources. They allowed me to examine performance, camera position, editing, and tone rather than relying only on written summaries. NZ On Screen also provided interviews and background essays. Those materials were useful, but they are curated accounts and should not be confused with the works themselves.

NZHistory and Te Ara were most useful for checking dates and statistics. I used them to confirm the chart history of "Poi E," the closure of the Pātea freezing works, and the scale of the 1981 protests. Te Papa's account of *Te Māori* was especially useful because it described both government involvement and Māori participation. This prevented me from treating the exhibition as a purely state-controlled project.

Papers Past was less useful for this part of the assignment. My searches produced little direct evidence about the production of the two works. It could still be valuable for studying contemporary public reaction, but that would require a more systematic search of individual newspapers and dates. Elections NZ and Stats NZ were more useful to group members working on politics and the economy than to my cultural topic.

The main limitation of my research is the lack of archival records. I did not consult production files, police warrants, broadcasting correspondence, or the original funding decision connected with the Pātea Māori Club's overseas tour. Some claims therefore depend on later interviews and institutional summaries. I have tried to show where the evidence is firm and where it is incomplete. This matters because the report itself argues that institutions shape the historical record.

### Conclusion

The history of "Poi E" and *Patu!* is not a simple story of the state rejecting Māori culture. It is a story of uneven access to national platforms. Audiences embraced "Poi E" even when the music industry hesitated. *Patu!* preserved a protest perspective that national television did not broadcast until years later. *Te Māori* showed that official support could also work with Māori leadership.

Their significance lies in the questions they raised about who could represent New Zealand. They also show that national culture is shaped through decisions about funding, broadcasting, and preservation. Later recognition confirms this change in public memory. "Poi E" returned to the charts in 2010, while *Patu!* entered the New Zealand UNESCO Memory of the World Register in 2012.[^10]
